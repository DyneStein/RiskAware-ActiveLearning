===============================================================================
WHAT WE FOUND
===============================================================================

Short version, in plain language. The numbers below all come from files in
this repository -- every one is checkable.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE QUESTION
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An AI that reads skin lesion photos will sometimes be confidently wrong. When
it is confidently wrong about a melanoma, a normal system waves that case
through without a second look, because "confident" is the only thing it checks.

We added a second check: how dangerous is this case if the AI is wrong? A case
gets sent to a human doctor if EITHER check fires -- if the AI is unsure, OR if
the case looks dangerous.

The question is whether that second check earns its keep.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT WE RAN
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

36 experiments in total, 15 rounds each, all on the same 1,905 held-out test
images:

   24  the original study: 3 network types x 4 ways of measuring the AI's
       uncertainty x 2 decision rules (ours and the standard one)
   12  four published methods from the literature, on all 3 network types:
       CoreSet, BADGE, CLUE and VAAL

Networks: ResNet-50, DenseNet-169, EfficientNet-B4.
Dataset: HAM10000, 10,015 dermoscopy images, 7 diagnosis types.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 1 -- FEWER DANGEROUS CASES WAVED THROUGH  (the main result)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Counting high-risk cases that were auto-accepted with no human review, added
up over all 15 rounds. Lower is better.

    Network            Ours    CoreSet   BADGE    CLUE     VAAL   Standard
    ---------------- -------  --------  ------  ------  -------  ---------
    ResNet-50          4,945     9,575   8,194   8,481   12,628      9,327
    DenseNet-169       4,495     8,543   6,947   7,397   11,308      8,275
    EfficientNet-B4    7,362     9,893  10,963  11,873   12,745     12,346

Our method wins all 15 comparisons, by between 26% and 61%. There is no
network and no competing method where it loses.

Against the standard uncertainty-only rule specifically, the reduction is
about 43% on average, and it holds in 12 out of 12 pairings.

File: paper/COMPARISON/tables/02_safety_scoreboard.csv
Figure: paper/COMPARISON/figures/fig1_safety_headline.png


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 2 -- ACCURACY DOES NOT SUFFER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Compared image by image on the 1,905 test images, and corrected for the fact
that we made 15 comparisons at once:

    versus VAAL                     better by 2.6 to 5.4 points   (real)
    versus uncertainty-only, B4     better by 2.4 points          (real)
    versus CoreSet, BADGE, CLUE     between -0.7 and +1.5 points  (no difference)

"No difference" is the intended outcome here, not a disappointment. CoreSet,
BADGE and CLUE are built specifically to squeeze the most learning out of each
label. Matching them on accuracy while cutting dangerous auto-accepts roughly
in half is the point.

File: paper/COMPARISON/tables/03_significance_image_level.csv


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 3 -- THE DANGER SCORE ITSELF IS GOOD
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before it is used to make any decision, the danger score is asked one question:
can it separate cancerous lesions from harmless ones? It scores 0.962 on a
scale where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect.

So the signal is real. It is not an artefact of the decision rule.

File: paper/04_TABLES/risk_auroc_by_experiment.csv


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 4 -- BOTH SIGNALS ARE NEEDED
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Of the high-risk images in the pool:

    escalating at random catches       10.2%
    uncertainty alone catches          12.6%    (barely better than random,
                                                 and it spent 4,339 labels)
    both signals together catch        29.3%

The two signals pick out largely different images. That contrast is the single
strongest argument for the design.

File: paper/04_TABLES/ablation_decision_level.csv


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT COSTS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

About 9% more cases sent to the human doctor (roughly 382 extra out of 4,678).

Being straight about this: at an equal number of labels, our method is about
0.35 points BEHIND on accuracy. So this is a safety improvement with a known
price, not a way of learning faster from fewer labels. Anyone claiming the
latter would be caught immediately.

File: paper/04_TABLES/al_efficiency_budget_matched.csv


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE LIMITATION THAT MATTERS MOST
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Finding 1 is measured on the pool of unlabelled images -- the cases the
decision rule actually decides about. That is the right place to measure a
decision rule, and we stand behind it.

But it is NOT the same claim as "the finished model misdiagnoses fewer new
patients". We measured that too, on the held-out test set, and while it moved
in our favour it did not reach statistical significance. Of 349 cancerous test
images our method catches 264 to 280 depending on the network, and against most
competitors that difference is one or two cases -- indistinguishable from luck.

We report this as a limitation rather than burying it. The likely reason is
identified: the two checks share the same underlying network, so they tend to
fail on the same hard images. Separating them is the obvious next step.

Two other honest limits:

  * Single seed. Every configuration was run once, not averaged over repeats,
    on the supervisor's instruction. The main safety result is consistent
    enough across 15 independent comparisons to be trusted; the weaker results
    would benefit from repeats.

  * Two of the seven diagnosis classes have very few test images (9 and 14).
    Confidence intervals for those classes are not trustworthy and are marked
    as such rather than quoted.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IN ONE SENTENCE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Adding a "how dangerous is this?" check alongside the usual "how sure is the
AI?" check cuts the number of dangerous cases waved through without review by
roughly a quarter to a half, costs about 9% more expert time, and does not hurt
accuracy -- across every network and every competing method we tested.
